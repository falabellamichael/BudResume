Executive Summary

Cannabis sativa L. represents one of the most chemically complex plants known to science, with over 565 distinct compounds identified to date [1][4]. This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the cannabis chemosphere, focusing on the major and minor cannabinoids—THC, CBD, CBG, CBN, THC-V, THC-A, and dozens more—alongside terpenes, flavonoids, alkaloids, and other bioactive constituents. We examine their biosynthesis, receptor pharmacology, entourage effects, therapeutic potential, toxicity profiles, and regulatory status. The science reveals a remarkably intricate biochemical factory where a single genetic pathway produces over 150 phytocannabinoids, each with unique and often synergistic interactions with human endocannabinoid, serotonergic, dopaminergic, and other receptor systems [3][7]. This report synthesizes current peer-reviewed literature to map the full chemical landscape of cannabis, providing molecular structures and mechanisms of action grounded in primary research.

1. Background and Context

1.1 The Botanical and Historical Framework

Cannabis sativa L. (family Cannabaceae) is a dioecious annual flowering plant that has accompanied human civilization for millennia, valued as a source of fiber (hemp), medicine, and psychoactive preparations [1][2]. The plant's chemical complexity was not appreciated until the mid-20th century, when Raphael Mechoulam and colleagues first isolated and structurally characterized Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) in 1964 and cannabidiol (CBD) in 1963 [6]. Since then, analytical advances—particularly gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy—have revealed a vast and diverse chemical inventory [4].

The current consensus identifies more than 565 distinct chemical entities in cannabis, broadly categorized as:

  • Cannabinoids (≥ 150 compounds, unique to Cannabis sativa)
  • Terpenes and terpenoids (≥ 200 compounds, shared with many plants)
  • Flavonoids (≥ 30 compounds, including cannflavins unique to cannabis)
  • Alkaloids (trace amounts, structurally distinct from typical plant alkaloids)
  • Phenolic compounds, stilbenoids, lignans
  • Fatty acids, sterols, waxes, and simple hydrocarbons [1][4]

1.2 Trichomes: The Phytochemical Factories

The vast majority of cannabis bioactive compounds are synthesized and stored in glandular trichomes—microscopic mushroom-shaped epidermal outgrowths most densely concentrated on the bracts and bracteoles of female inflorescences (commonly called "buds") [1]. Three trichome types exist:

Trichome Type Size Cannabinoid Content Description
Bulbous 10–30 μm Low Smallest, unicellular stalk
Capitate-sessile 20–100 μm Moderate Multicellular head, no stalk elongation
Capitate-stalked 150–500 μm Highest Large secretory head on multicellular stalk; primary site of cannabinoid and terpene synthesis

The secretory cavity within the glandular head contains a viscous resin rich in cannabinoid acids (the biosynthetic precursors), terpenes, and flavonoids [1][4]. This anatomical compartmentalization is critical: the decarboxylation of cannabinoid acids to their neutral (active) forms occurs only upon heating (smoking, vaporization, baking), aging, or extraction under acidic conditions.

2. The Complete Cannabinoid Inventory: Structures, Biosynthesis, and Pharmacology

2.1 Fundamental Biosynthetic Pathway

All phytocannabinoids share a common biosynthetic origin rooted in two metabolic pathways: the polyketide pathway (producing the olivetolic acid or divarinic acid moieties) and the methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway (producing the geranyl pyrophosphate terpenoid moiety) [5][6].

The canonical pathway proceeds as follows:

  1. Hexanoyl-CoA (from fatty acid degradation) or butyryl-CoA combines with three malonyl-CoA molecules via olivetol synthase (OLS) and olivetolic acid cyclase (OAC) to form olivetolic acid (OA)—a resorcinolic core with a pentyl (C5) side chain. Alternatively, the same enzymes using butyryl-CoA yield divarinic acid (DA) with a propyl (C3) side chain [5].
  1. Geranyl pyrophosphate (GPP) from the MEP pathway is coupled to olivetolic acid by the prenyltransferase enzyme geranyl pyrophosphate:olivetolate geranyltransferase (GOT) to yield cannabigerolic acid (CBGA) —the "mother cannabinoid" from which all subsequent major phytocannabinoids derive [5][6].
  1. CBGA then serves as substrate for three principal oxidocyclase enzymes:
  • Tetrahydrocannabinolic acid synthase (THCAS) → Δ⁹-THCA
  • Cannabidiolic acid synthase (CBDAS) → CBDA
  • Cannabichromenic acid synthase (CBCAS) → CBCA

These oxidocyclases catalyze stereospecific, non-oxidative cyclization of the geranyl moiety, each producing a distinct tricyclic scaffold [5].

  1. The acidic cannabinoids (THCA, CBDA, CBCA, and CBGA itself) undergo non-enzymatic decarboxylation upon heating (T > 105°C), aging, or exposure to light, losing CO₂ to yield the neutral forms: Δ⁹-THC, CBD, CBC, and CBG, respectively [5]. In the plant, decarboxylation is minimal; fresh cannabis contains predominantly acidic forms.
  1. Side-chain variants: Utilization of divarinic acid instead of olivetolic acid yields the corresponding propyl (C3) homologs: tetrahydrocannabivarin acid (THCVA), cannabidivarin acid (CBDVA), etc., with the neutral forms THC-V, CBD-V, and so on [5].
  1. Degradation products: Neutral cannabinoids such as THC are labile. Oxidation converts THC to cannabinol (CBN) ; prolonged storage or heating in the presence of oxygen accelerates this. Isomerization of Δ⁹-THC to Δ⁸-THC occurs under acidic conditions [1].

A simplified schematic:

Hexanoyl-CoA ──(OLS/OAC)──> Olivetolic acid ─┐
                                               ├──(GOT)──> CBGA ──(THCAS)──> THCA ──(Δ)──> Δ⁹-THC ──(ox)──> CBN
Geranyl-PP ───────────────────────────────────┘           ├──(CBDAS)──> CBDA ──(Δ)──> CBD
                                                           ├──(CBCAS)──> CBCA ──(Δ)──> CBC
                                                           └─(Δ)──> CBG

2.2 Major Cannabinoids: Detailed Profiles

2.2.1 Δ⁹-Tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ⁹-THC)

Property Detail
Molecular formula C₂₁H₃₀O₂
Molecular weight 314.46 g/mol
IUPAC name (–)-(6aR,10aR)-6,6,9-trimethyl-3-pentyl-6a,7,8,10a-tetrahydro-6H-benzo[c]chromen-1-ol
Biosynthetic precursor Δ⁹-THCA (decarboxylated)
Primary receptors CB1 partial agonist (Ki ~ 10 nM); CB2 partial agonist (weaker)
Psychoactivity Classic euphoriant, principal intoxicant of cannabis
Key therapeutic targets Pain, nausea/vomiting, appetite stimulation, muscle spasticity, glaucoma

Mechanism of Action: Δ⁹-THC is a partial agonist at cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1) and type 2 (CB2). At CB1 receptors predominantly located in the central nervous system (cortex, hippocampus, cerebellum, basal ganglia), THC inhibits adenylyl cyclase via Gᵢ/o proteins, reduces calcium influx through voltage-gated channels, and enhances potassium efflux, collectively suppressing neurotransmitter release (GABA, glutamate, dopamine) [3][7]. This presynaptic inhibition underlies the complex biphasic effects: low doses can be anxiolytic and analgesic, while higher doses may provoke anxiety, paranoia, and psychosis-like symptoms.

THC's psychoactive potency is modulated by its partial agonist character; full CB1 agonists (synthetic cannabinoids like JWH-018) often produce severe toxicity due to supraphysiological receptor activation [7]. THC also activates GPR55 (a putative "CB3" receptor), transient receptor potential (TRP) channels (TRPV2, TRPV3, TRPA1, TRPM8) with lower affinity, and allosterically modulates opioid and serotonin receptors [3][7].

Metabolism: Hepatic CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 convert THC to 11-hydroxy-THC (approximately equi-potent to THC) and subsequently to 11-nor-9-carboxy-THC (inactive, water-soluble, excreted in urine). The 11-hydroxy metabolite crosses the blood-brain barrier more readily and is primarily responsible for the delayed, more intense high from oral ingestion (edibles) compared to inhalation [6].

2.2.2 Cannabidiol (CBD)

Property Detail
Molecular formula C₂₁H₃₀O₂
Molecular weight 314.46 g/mol
IUPAC name 2-[(1R,6R)-3-methyl-6-(prop-1-en-2-yl)cyclohex-2-en-1-yl]-5-pentylbenzene-1,3-diol
Biosynthetic precursor CBDA
Primary receptors Low CB1/CB2 affinity; negative allosteric modulator (NAM) of CB1; agonist at 5-HT1A, TRPV1, GPR55 antagonist
Psychoactivity None; non-intoxicating; may attenuate THC-induced anxiety

Mechanism of Action: CBD is pharmacologically promiscuous, interacting with over 65 molecular targets, yet it lacks significant direct orthosteric binding to CB1 or CB2. Instead, key mechanisms include [3][7]:

  • Negative allosteric modulator of CB1: Binds to a distinct (non-orthosteric) site on CB1, reducing potency and efficacy of orthosteric agonists like THC and endocannabinoids. This provides a molecular basis for CBD's ability to mitigate THC-induced tachycardia, anxiety, and psychotic symptoms [3].
  • Agonist at 5-HT1A (serotonin receptor, Ki ~ 16 μM): Contributes to anxiolytic, antidepressant, and neuroprotective effects.
  • Agonist at TRPV1-4 (vanilloid receptors): Underlies analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-convulsant properties.
  • Antagonist at GPR55: Linked to anti-cancer (anti-proliferative) and anti-inflammatory actions.
  • Inverse agonist at CB2 (at high concentrations): Modest immunomodulatory effects.
  • Inhibitor of FAAH (fatty acid amide hydrolase): Slows breakdown of anandamide (endocannabinoid), elevating anandamide tone, indirectly enhancing CB1-mediated signaling.

Clinical Translation: Epidiolex® (purified plant-derived CBD) is FDA-approved for Dravet syndrome, Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, and tuberous sclerosis complex-associated seizures. CBD also demonstrates antipsychotic, anxiolytic, and anti-inflammatory promise [6][7].

2.2.3 Cannabigerol (CBG)

Property Detail
Molecular formula C₂₁H₃₂O₂
Molecular weight 316.48 g/mol
IUPAC name 2-[(2E)-3,7-dimethylocta-2,6-dienyl]-5-pentylbenzene-1,3-diol
Biosynthetic precursor CBGA (decarboxylated)
Primary receptors CB1 partial agonist (Ki ~ 400 nM); CB2 partial agonist; α₂-adrenergic agonist; TRPV1, TRPA1 agonist; 5-HT1A antagonist
Psychoactivity Non-psychoactive (weak CB1 agonism insufficient to cause intoxication)

CBG is often termed the "stem cell" or "mother" cannabinoid because CBGA is the direct precursor to THCA, CBDA, and CBCA [1][5]. In most modern drug-type (high-THC) or hemp-type (high-CBD) cultivars, CBGA is almost completely converted, resulting in low (<1%) final CBG content. Breeders have recently developed "CBG-dominant" chemotypes with disrupted or absent THCAS and CBDAS genes, yielding plants rich in CBG [5].

Pharmacology: CBG shows moderate affinity for both CB receptors, with slightly higher CB2 binding. It acts as an α₂-adrenoceptor agonist (Ki ~ 0.2 nM), suggesting roles in modulating sympathetic tone (potentially relevant for glaucoma: reduced intraocular pressure via vasoconstriction). CBG is a potent TRPV1 and TRPA1 agonist, contributing to anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects. It also antagonizes 5-HT1A, contrasting with CBD's agonism there, and blocks CB1 with low potency. Remarkably, CBG demonstrates strong antibacterial activity against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and inhibits keratinocyte proliferation, suggesting potential in psoriasis and other dermatological conditions [3][7].

2.2.4 Cannabinol (CBN)

Property Detail
Molecular formula C₂₁H₂₆O₂
Molecular weight 310.43 g/mol
IUPAC name 6,6,9-trimethyl-3-pentylbenzo[c]chromen-1-ol
Biosynthetic origin Oxidation/aromatization of Δ⁹-THC
Primary receptors CB1 partial agonist (Ki ~ 210 nM, ~10-fold weaker than THC); CB2 partial agonist
Psychoactivity Weakly psychoactive (estimated 10–25% of THC potency)

CBN is not enzymatically produced in cannabis; it is an oxidative degradation product of THC. Fresh, properly stored cannabis contains negligible CBN. High CBN concentrations indicate aged, poorly stored, or heat-degraded material (e.g., cannabis left exposed to air and light for months) [1][5]. The aromatization of the monoterpene ring (formation of a benzene ring) renders CBN less potent as a CB1 agonist but stable to further oxidation.

Metabolic Insights: CBN demonstrates mild sedative effects in some studies, which has led to claims that "aged cannabis causes couch-lock." However, pure CBN's sedative properties remain equivocal and may be confounded by terpene co-factors (notably myrcene, which is a recognized sedative) [3][7]. CBN also has TRPV2 agonist activity and inhibits keratinocyte proliferation, together with anti-inflammatory effects, sparking interest in topical applications for burns, eczema, and psoriasis.

2.2.5 Δ⁹-Tetrahydrocannabivarin (THC-V)

Property Detail
Molecular formula C₁₉H₂₆O₂
Molecular weight 286.41 g/mol
IUPAC name (6aR,10aR)-6,6,9-trimethyl-3-propyl-6a,7,8,10a-tetrahydro-6H-benzo[c]chromen-1-ol
Biosynthetic precursor THCVA (derived from divarinic acid instead of olivetolic acid)
Primary receptors CB1 neutral antagonist/weak inverse agonist (low doses); CB1 partial agonist (high doses); CB2 partial agonist
Psychoactivity Biphasic: low doses may attenuate THC effects; high doses produce mild euphoria

THC-V is the propyl (C3 side chain) analog of THC. The shortened side chain profoundly alters pharmacology: at low concentrations (< 1 μM), THC-V behaves as a CB1 neutral antagonist, blocking the effects of THC and endocannabinoids without exerting inverse agonism (unlike rimonabant). This can suppress appetite and reduce THC-induced tachycardia [3][7]. At high concentrations (> 10 μM), THC-V engages CB1 as a partial agonist, producing mild psychoactive effects.

This biphasic pharmacology has generated enthusiasm for THC-V as a potential weight-loss agent (CB1 blockade reduces food intake) and anti-diabetic (improves insulin sensitivity and reduces pancreatic β-cell inflammation in rodent models). THC-V also demonstrates anticonvulsant properties in animal seizure models and may reduce tremor in Parkinson's disease models [7].

2.2.6 Δ⁹-Tetrahydrocannabinolic Acid (THCA)

Property Detail
Molecular formula C₂₂H₃₀O₄
Molecular weight 358.47 g/mol
IUPAC name (6aR,10aR)-1-hydroxy-6,6,9-trimethyl-3-pentyl-6a,7,8,10a-tetrahydro-6H-benzo[c]chromene-2-carboxylic acid
Biosynthetic role Immediate precursor to Δ⁹-THC; product of THCAS acting on CBGA
Primary receptors Very low affinity for CB1/CB2; COX-1/2 inhibitor; agonist at TRPA1, TRPV3; PPARγ modulator
Psychoactivity None (does not cross blood-brain barrier efficiently; does not activate CB1)

THCA is the most abundant cannabinoid in fresh, unheated cannabis flower (often 15–30% by dry weight in high-THC chemotypes). It is a carboxylic acid; decarboxylation (loss of CO₂) upon heating transforms it into psychoactive THC. Because THCA is anionic at physiological pH, it has poor membrane permeability and does not readily cross the blood-brain barrier. Consequently, eating raw cannabis does not produce intoxication [1][5].

THCA possesses its own pharmacological activity independent of CB receptors. It inhibits cyclooxygenase enzymes (COX-1 and COX-2) with moderate potency, suggesting anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects via the prostaglandin pathway. THCA also activates TRPA1 and TRPV3 channels, contributing to anti-inflammatory and anti-nociceptive actions. Emerging evidence points to PPARγ (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma) agonism, with implications for metabolic syndrome, neuroprotection, and anti-cancer activity [3][7]. THCA shows anti-emetic effects in rodent models, possibly via 5-HT1A and CB1-independent mechanisms.

2.4 Key Minor Cannabinoids in Depth

Cannabichromene (CBC)

  • Biosynthesis: CBCAS cyclizes CBGA to CBCA, then decarboxylates to CBC. Typically third-most abundant cannabinoid in high-THC cultivars (0.1–0.5%) [5].
  • Pharmacology: Weak CB1 agonist; stronger CB2 activity. Potent TRPA1 agonist (antinociceptive, anti-inflammatory). Inhibits anandamide reuptake (IC50 ~ 20 μM), elevating endocannabinoid tone, which may underpin its synergistic analgesia with THC [3]. CBC promotes neurogenesis in adult neural stem/progenitor cells in vitro, though in vivo relevance is unclear.

Cannabidivarin (CBDV)

  • Propyl analog of CBD. Predominantly in Cannabis indica landraces and some feral populations.
  • Pharmacology: Similar to CBD but more potent at TRPV1 and TRPA1. Strong anticonvulsant in rodent models (Dravet syndrome, PTZ-induced seizures); Phase II trials for epilepsy and autism spectrum disorder (GW Pharmaceuticals) underway [7].

Cannabigerovarin (CBGV)

  • Propyl analog of CBG. Present in some Asian and African landraces. Under-explored but anticipated to share CBG pharmacology with subtle kinetic differences.

Δ⁸-Tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ⁸-THC)

  • Isomer of Δ⁹-THC with double bond shifted from position 9–10 to 8–9. More chemically stable than Δ⁹-THC. Occurs in trace amounts naturally but is typically a synthetic artifact from acid-catalyzed isomerization of CBD [5].
  • Pharmacology: CB1 partial agonist, slightly lower affinity than Δ⁹-THC. Reported to produce a "clearer" high with less anxiety. Legal status varies; 2018 Farm Bill loophole led to widespread semi-synthetic Δ⁸-THC products from hemp-derived CBD, raising safety and purity concerns due to unregulated synthesis byproducts [6].

Cannabichromevarin (CBCV)

  • Propyl CBC analog. Trace compound; pharmacology unknown beyond weak TRP modulation.

Tetrahydrocannabiphorol (THCP) and Cannabidiphorol (CBDP)

  • Recently discovered (2019) in Italian FM2 medical cannabis variety. Heptyl (C7) side chain rather than pentyl (C5). THCP shows 33× higher CB1 affinity (Ki ~ 1.2 nM vs. THC's ~ 40 nM) and markedly greater in vivo cannabimimetic activity in rodent tetrad assays [5]. Implications: some heavily sedating cannabis chemovars may owe potency to trace heptyl cannabinoids. Detection requires specialized LC-MS methods.

Cannabimovone (CBM)

  • A rearranged abeo-cannabinoid with a cyclopentyl motif; weak CB1 activity but potentiates THC effects. Possible entourage compound.

3. Terpenes and Terpenoids: The Aromatic Orchestra

3.1 Overview and Classification

Cannabis produces over 200 terpenes and terpenoids, volatile isoprenoid compounds responsible for the plant's characteristic aromas and flavors. Terpenes are classified by the number of isoprene (C5H8) units [1][4]:

  • Monoterpenes (C10H16): Most abundant; highly volatile; dominate fresh flower aroma. Examples: myrcene, limonene, α-pinene, β-pinene, linalool, terpinolene, β-ocimene.
  • Sesquiterpenes (C15H24): Less volatile; contribute to "skunky," spicy, or woody notes. Examples: β-caryophyllene, α-humulene, β-farnesene, bisabolene, guaiol, caryophyllene oxide.
  • Diterpenes (C20H32): Phytol (chlorophyll side chain), geranylgeraniol; minor.
  • Triterpenes (C30H48): Friedelin, epifriedelanol; trace; contribute to waxes.

Terpenes are synthesized in the same glandular trichome secretory cells as cannabinoids but via the MEP (plastidial) or mevalonate (cytosolic) pathways, and then accumulate in the resin alongside cannabinoids [4].

3.3 The Entourage Effect: Terpene-Cannabinoid Synergy

The "entourage effect" postulates that cannabis constituents act synergistically, such that whole-plant extracts produce therapeutic outcomes distinct from or superior to isolated compounds at equivalent doses [1][3][7]. While often invoked anecdotally, rigorous pharmacological evidence is accumulating:

  • β-Caryophyllene's CB2 agonism: Enhances anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory actions of CBD/CBG without psychotropic effects [3].
  • Myrcene's membrane perturbation: In rodent models, myrcene pre-treatment increases THC brain concentrations, potentially by enhancing blood-brain barrier permeability or inhibiting metabolic clearance. However, human data are scarce [7].
  • Limonene and linalool's serotonergic/GABAergic augmentation: Directly counterbalance THC's anxiety and dysphoria via receptor-level interactions on distinct but functionally connected neural circuits.
  • α-Pinene's AChE inhibition: Attenuation of THC-induced short-term memory impairment via preserved hippocampal acetylcholine levels observed in radial maze tasks in rats.

Criticisms: The effect sizes in many entourage claims are small; whole-plant extracts contain variable, unstandardized compositions precluding reproducibility. Pharmaceutical co-formulations (e.g., nabiximols: Sativex® fixed THC:CBD ratio) represent partial acceptance, yet inclusion of specific terpenes remains contentious.

4. Phenolics, Flavonoids, Stilbenoids, and Lignans

Cannabis synthesizes a rich polyphenolic profile contributing to color, antioxidant capacity, and potential bioactivity [1][4].

4.1 Flavonoids

Flavonoids are polyphenolic compounds with a C6–C3–C6 backbone. Cannabis contains at least 30 flavonoids, including several prenylated flavonoids (cannflavins) unique to the genus [4].

Flavonoid Subclass Examples Notes
Flavones Apigenin, luteolin, vitexin, orientin, cosmosiin Anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anxiolytic (apigenin is a benzodiazepine-site ligand on GABA-A)
Flavonols Kaempferol, quercetin, myricetin, rutin Strong antioxidants; quercetin inhibits CYP3A4, potentially altering cannabinoid metabolism
Flavanones Naringenin, eriodictyol, hesperetin Minor; contribute to citrus and bitter notes
Cannflavins (prenylated flavones) Cannflavin A, Cannflavin B, Cannflavin C Unique to Cannabis sativa; geranyl or dimethylallyl substituents. Cannflavin A is a potent prostaglandin E2 synthesis inhibitor (IC50 ~ 0.5 μM; 30× more potent than aspirin in vitro). Anti-inflammatory, possible CYP450 interactions

Cannflavins are concentrated in the leaves and inflorescence, with cannflavin A content reaching up to 0.2% dry weight in some cultivars. They represent a promising non-cannabinoid anti-inflammatory scaffold for drug discovery, circumventing CB1-mediated psychoactivity [4].

4.2 Stilbenoids

Stilbenoids share a C6–C2–C6 backbone. Cannabis produces several, including resveratrol (famous from grapes) but more notably cannabistilbenes I, II, and III with prenyl modifications. Activities: antioxidant, antifungal, potential cancer chemopreventive [1].

4.3 Lignans and Phenolic Amides

Trace lignans (e.g., pinoresinol, lariciresinol) and phenolic amides (N-trans-coumaroyltyramine, N-trans-feruloyltyramine) are present. These contribute to cell wall structure and defense, and possess antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of unclear systemic relevance after consumption.

5. Alkaloids

Alkaloids are nitrogenous bases typically associated with potent pharmacological activity (morphine, caffeine, nicotine). Cannabis is remarkable for its paucity of alkaloids—a feature distinguishing it from other psychoactive plants like Erythroxylum (cocaine) or Papaver (opioids) [4].

Identified cannabis alkaloids include:

  • Cannabisativine: A spermidine alkaloid; weakly anticholinergic.
  • Anhydrocannabisativine: Related structure.
  • Choline and trigonelline: Common in many plants; precursors to neurotransmitter acetylcholine, methyl donor pathways. Choline concentrations in cannabis seed are relatively high (nutritional significance).
  • Muscarine-like and hordenine-like trace bases (inconclusive structural verification).

Total alkaloid content is exceptionally low (< 0.01% dry weight). No psychoactive or significant pharmacological effects are attributed to cannabis alkaloids at typical consumption levels. Their biosynthetic origin appears convergent with polyamine metabolism rather than dedicated alkaloid pathways.

6. Other Chemical Classes

6.1 Steroids and Triterpenoids

Phytosterols: β-sitosterol, campesterol, stigmasterol (common in plant membranes). Potential to modestly lower serum LDL-cholesterol when consumed as seed oil (hemp seed oil). Triterpenoids (friedelin, epifriedelanol) in leaf waxes [1].

6.2 Fatty Acids and Seed Oil Composition

Hemp seeds (from low-THC cannabis varieties) yield a nutritionally valuable oil rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), with an omega-6:omega-3 ratio of approximately 3:1, considered optimal for human nutrition. Key fatty acids [6]:
